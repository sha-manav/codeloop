Building a self-improving medical-coding agent

How a certified coder's corrections became findings, findings became evals, and an improvement agent climbed them inside a gated loop: three cycles, four frozen versions, one repository, every step in a ledger.

Built with a certified professional coder (CPC) on 175 scripted outpatient encounters · 3,021 review events · 103 findings · 3 gated merges · about $100 of model calls

Real-world coding systems fail in ways that are hard to anticipate before deployment. A draft that looks complete to an engineer is wrong to a coder for reasons that only surface once someone who bills for a living corrects it. Teams typically discover those failures after launch, then spend weeks reading traces, adjusting prompts, and translating feedback into durable fixes. The loop is manual and slow, and it only moves when an engineer moves it.

CodeLoop is an outpatient professional-coding agent wrapped in a harness that turns a coder's corrections into structured evidence, evidence into targeted evals, and evals into bounded engineering tasks for an improvement agent. The agent reads the visit transcript and the clinical note and drafts a coding package: ICD-10-CM diagnoses with specificity, laterality and active-versus-historical status, in-scope imaging lines with modifiers, units and diagnosis pointers, and an evidence span for every field. A CPC reviews each draft in a web tool. Changes to the pipeline merge only through a gate, and a human approves each one.

Over three cycles the harness produced three new frozen versions, v1 through v3, from the original v0. Each cycle's version drafted a fresh batch of 45 encounters, the coder reviewed all 45, and the drafts he had to change shrank with each version.

Measurable self-improvement

We quantify a draft by how much of it the coder keeps. After review, every field of the approved label is compared with the draft, and we report the share of encounters whose fields were at least 75%, at least 90%, or 100% in agreement. The thresholds give a practical view of how much follow-up a draft still needs: an encounter at 100% needed no correction at all.

At cycle 0, half of the drafts cleared the 75% tier and under a third were fully correct. By cycle 2, 87% cleared 75% and 62% needed no change. The 90% and 100% tiers moved fastest, from 29% to 67% and 62%.

The coder's effort moved the same way. A touch is one edit, addition or removal he made to a draft; touches per encounter fell from 1.82 to 0.69, and zero-touch encounters went from 13 of 45 to 28 of 45. Mean review time fell from 4.4 to 2.7 minutes per encounter.

What the coder corrected also changed in kind from cycle to cycle, which is what you want from a loop: fixing one class of error exposes the next.

The problem

In the first cycle, most of the correction signal was invisible to engineering. The coder could fix a draft, but a changed code might mean a genuine miss, a candidate-lookup gap, a coding guideline the pipeline did not encode, or the coder's own judgment call. A removed code might be wrong, or unbillable, or simply not something this coder codes for this kind of visit. Sorting those apart still required an engineer reading traces one encounter at a time.

The harder question was structural: could the product make its own failures visible, grouped and actionable, so that a coding agent could act on them without an engineer standing in the middle of every fix? We did not have the signal to identify the right hill to climb.

Our approach: a three-part loop

1Stay close to the practitionerThe coder's corrections steer what the product learns. His guidelines, not ours, define what a visit is coded for. When a pattern was ambiguous, the question went back to him rather than through the loop.
2Build the product so review creates evidenceEvery action in the review tool is an append-only event with the field, the before and after values and a reason: missed, unsupported, specificity, wrong value, guideline, or judgment. Labels are rebuilt by replaying events, so nothing is lost and nothing can be quietly changed.
3Create an agent-driven improvement loopRecurring corrections become findings; findings become targeted evals; an improvement agent investigates inside a bounded task environment, changes the pipeline, validates against targeted and regression suites, and opens a pull request. A gate decides what may merge. A human merges.

Missed-diabetes example

Type 2 diabetes is among the most common chronic conditions in these visits. Coding it is simple to describe and easy to get wrong: find the condition in the note, choose the right ICD-10-CM code from a category with dozens of complication-specific members, and keep enough evidence that a coder can approve or correct the choice. The example below walks one correction through the loop.

1. A practitioner correction reveals a failure

In cycle 1 the coder added the type 2 diabetes code E11.9 to four drafts that did not have it, each with the reason missed. All four notes named the condition. A difference between draft and label can mean many things, so the harness does not act on one correction; a pattern must recur in at least three encounters of a batch, or two if it was seen in an earlier batch, before it becomes a finding. Four of the same correction cleared the threshold, and the harness raised FIND-DX-0052: missed corrections on diagnosis fields, E11.

2. Product traces turn corrections into evals

Every draft carries a full trace: what the extractor listed as problems, which candidate codes the lookup offered for each, what the mapper selected and the rationale it gave. That makes it possible to locate a failure rather than guess at it. To turn corrections into an evaluation target the harness works in three steps:

  • Capture the difference. Each edit, addition or removal is one event with the field reference, before and after values, and the coder's reason. No-op edits, where the coder pressed Edit without changing anything, are detected by replay and excluded.
  • Group related failures. Events are grouped by reason, field type and ICD-10-CM category. Self-corrections, like a code added then removed a minute later, are noted at triage so they do not count as product failures.
  • Turn repeated patterns into eval targets. An accepted finding is packaged into a targeted dataset (its encounters, the coder's labels as gold, and the exact fields that must be right) plus a regression suite of every labeled encounter so far, three seeded runs each.

3. The finding becomes a hill to climb

The improvement agent works inside a container that mounts the repository with the sealed data absent, the scorer, compliance rules and configuration read-only, and network egress only to the model provider through an allowlist proxy. It can read code, prompts and traces, run the two suites, and change a defined set of files. It cannot edit the tests, the datasets, or the findings.

It read the four traces and located the cause in the lookup step, not in the model. Then it worked the hill in three attempts, each recorded in the ledger:

If the evidence is ambiguous, the case routes back to a human. In cycle 2 the only eligible finding was set aside because the coder had both kept and replaced the same symptom code in the same batch, with the same documentation shape. It went to him as a plain question. He answered, "I usually code the cause if the cause is documented," and only then did the finding become a task.

How the loop is built

A correction does not become a task automatically. It may be a miss, a lookup gap, a guideline the pipeline does not encode, or the coder's judgment. Only after repeated corrections have been grouped and triaged does the harness produce a bounded task with a clear success condition. For the improvement agent, the result is not a vague alert but a scoped engineering task with evidence, editable surfaces and explicit validation gates:

Three conventions kept the loop honest at low cost:

  • The base is stored, not re-run. Every version's drafts for every labeled batch are committed for three seeds, so a gate compares the head against fixed numbers. When a version was frozen with only one seed stored, the missing seeds were run at the tag and committed before gating.
  • Failed attempts stay in the ledger. Ten gate runs are recorded across the three cycles, including the two that failed and one that was void because the base was mis-packaged; the fix to the packager is itself a commit.
  • Nothing merges without a person. The improvement agent opens the pull request; the owner merges it; the version is frozen with a tag and the ledger records the hashes.

Expanding across cycles

The same loop ran three times. Each cycle's finding was different in kind, and each fix held on the batch that followed it.

Across the three cycles the coder's 3,021 review events contained 154 effective corrections. Grouping produced 103 findings; seven cleared the recurrence threshold; six were accepted and resolved, and one was rejected after the coder's own labels showed he codes the examination finding in question consistently.

What made the loop cheap to run was not the agent but the plumbing around it: an append-only event store, a recurrence threshold, a packager that turns a finding into a dataset and two suites, a container that cannot reach what it must not, and a gate whose verdict is a file in the repository. Model spend across the three cycles, including the failed gate attempts and the sealed predictions for four versions, was about $100 by the ledger's own estimates, and less in practice because repeated runs hit the cache.

What we also checked

Before the first cycle, 40 encounters were sealed and never opened; at the end, the coder coded them with no draft in front of him, and all four versions were scored against those labels exactly once. On that blind set the versions did not differ from one another. The curve in Figure 1 therefore measures how much of a draft a reviewer accepts, which is what it was designed to measure, not from-scratch coding accuracy. The full analysis, including why the two measurements differ, is in the study note.

Encounters are scripted or role-played (ACI-Bench); there is no PHI. CPT descriptors are never stored; code numbers only. Every freeze, gate, merge and correction is recorded in ledger.md with hashes.
